# Related-work and novelty research

The objective is a defensible comparison with the closest prior art, not an arbitrary number of citations or an exhaustive-review claim. Use available web/scholarly tools; no specific paid service or API is required.

## Search scope and query design

1. State the search date, target field and contribution type, and whether assessing novelty as of today or a user-specified historical cutoff. Distinguish what was public before a historical cutoff from later work; later work may inform current viability without defeating an earlier claim of priority.
2. Translate each central contribution into query families: the question/problem, proposed mechanism, setting/constraints, and claimed interaction or combination. Include standard terms, acronyms expanded, synonyms, older names, and neighboring fields. Look for the same mechanism under a different name.
3. Search broad conceptual combinations first, then targeted formulations and counterclaims such as existing equivalents, known limitations, replications, benchmarks, or negative results. Do not depend on literal title matches. Search beyond the proposal's own references and favorable examples.
4. Use a scholarly index or appropriate field repository alongside broader web search when available: for example Semantic Scholar, OpenAlex, Crossref, PubMed, arXiv, ACL Anthology, or proceedings/publisher sites. Record the sources actually used; this list is not a required platform checklist. If only one retrieval source is available, disclose that coverage limit.
5. Inspect both foundational and recent work, including relevant public preprints and workshops. Do not equate unreviewed status with irrelevance to overlap; label the publication status. Follow references backward from the closest papers and citing/follow-up papers forward where tools permit. A recent survey can guide discovery but does not substitute for the primary work establishing the contested claim.

Use generic scientific terms and publicly documented concepts in queries. Do not paste proposal paragraphs, unpublished titles, personal identities, private dataset identifiers, sensitive numbers, or proprietary details into external services. Specific combinations of public concepts can still reveal a private direction: when that distinction matters, use broader discovery and inspect candidate sources locally, flagging any resulting coverage limit. Never upload the full proposal to an external novelty checker. A user's explicit confidentiality constraints govern the search scope.

## Inspect and verify sources

- Open the primary paper, official repository record, or publisher/proceedings page for each consequential comparison. Verify the title, authors, date/version, and stable URL/DOI where available. Report only metadata you actually checked; never invent a DOI, author list, venue, or publication status.
- Read the relevant methods/theorem, assumptions, evaluation, and limitations for the closest competitors when accessible. Mark **full text inspected**, **abstract only**, or **metadata only** per source. Do not assert a particular omission from a full paper based on its abstract.
- Prefer author manuscripts, official proceedings, institutional repositories, and publisher versions over secondary summaries. Search snippets, model memory, and news/blog explanations are discovery leads, not sufficient evidence of a decisive duplication or novelty claim.
- If access is blocked, look for a lawful public version. Keep inaccessible sources as unresolved candidates instead of silently excluding potential counterexamples. If the key novelty decision depends on them, lower confidence or withhold that decision.
- Deduplicate preprint and published versions as one work; note substantive version differences that affect overlap. Track the earliest verified public date rather than confusing publication year with first disclosure. Label a preprint as a preprint; journal publication does not retroactively establish a first-public date.
- When a claim concerns what a source did *not* address, state the scope inspected and use a bounded formulation. Cite sections, figures, or short relevant passages where the source provides them; do not manufacture locators or quote at length.

## Compare claims rather than keywords

For each closest work, compare:

- Research question, task, and claimed contribution.
- Mechanism/method or theoretical result and assumptions.
- Population, setting, data, resource, or constraints relevant to the distinction.
- Evidence and findings, including whether the proposal's intended experiment is already reported.
- Exact overlap, substantive remaining difference, and whether that difference matters scientifically.

Separate **direct precedent**, **partial overlap**, **complementary work**, and **unresolved candidate**. Two papers proposing components A and B separately are not evidence that their interaction has been studied. Conversely, juxtaposing A and B is not a contribution until the proposal explains a meaningful interaction, hypothesis, or capability and how to test it.

An "already done" finding needs a cited source establishing the same substantive claim under comparable assumptions, not just a similar title. Use a partial-overlap verdict when a competitor anticipates only part of the proposal. Novelty and priority concern scholarly contribution here; this workflow does not perform patent or legal clearance.

## Stop and report honestly

Continue until each central novelty claim has a relevant comparison (or an explicitly unresolved gap), major synonyms and neighboring formulations have been explored, and backward/forward expansion of the closest accessible work no longer changes the assessment within the available effort. Do not stop merely because a citation quota is met, or continue indefinitely to imply certainty.

Respect user time, tool, and search limits. If they stop the search early, or rate limits/access failures prevent needed checks, report **Partial** and list the unresolved branches. Do not describe an effort-limited search as saturated.

Include a compact search log: actual query or accurate sanitized paraphrase, retrieval source, date, useful hits, inspected sources, and remaining gaps. A number of results is optional and must come from the tool if reported. Use a concise source list with verified titles, dates, links, and access levels, cross-referenced from the comparison table. Citation count is not a quality or novelty score.

Conclude with a bounded statement: **direct precedent found for claim X**, **partial overlap**, **potentially differentiated within searched literature**, or **unresolved**. State what additional source or search would most likely change that conclusion.
